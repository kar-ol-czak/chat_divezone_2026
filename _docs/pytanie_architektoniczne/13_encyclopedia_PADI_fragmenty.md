## Sekcja: BCD (Buoyancy Control Devices) - linie 1033-1180

#### Buoyancy Control Devices (BCDs)

Non-existent in the mid 1960s and optional in the early 1970s, by the mid-to-late 1970s the BCD had become mandatory equipment for recreational divers. The modern BCD has three primary functions: 1) to let you float on the surface to rest or swim, 2) to allow you to adjust changes in your buoyancy while underwater, and 3) to act as the harness that holds the entire scuba system together on you.

Since, in theory, if you're properly weighted you shouldn't need a BCD to stay at the surface, you may wonder about the first point. It's because when you're properly weighted, you'll be almost entirely submerged at the surface except for the top of your head and snorkel. The additional flotation from a BCD gets your head well out of the water, which is often more relaxing. Also, you may overestimate how much weight you

> need. Finally, as you'll see, your weight changes based on the

amount of air in your scuba cylinder. At the start of a typical recreational dive, you should actually be about two kilograms/five pounds or so heavy when you're properly weighted.

During the dive, your wet suit compresses and expands as you descend and ascend. This changes its buoyancy, which you compensate for with your BCD so you remain neutrally buoyant throughout the dive. When you're diving with a dry suit, you do not use the BCD to adjust buoyancy underwater, but you still need the BCD for surface flotation and (more importantly) as a backup buoyancy device if your suit has a massive leak.

#### Materials and Construction

Manufacturers make two types of BCD.

The double bag design uses two layers: a tough, protective fabric outer layer that contains and protects an inside bladder that holds the air. The bladderless design uses a tough, airtight material to accomplish both jobs in one material.

Both double bag and bladderless construction have proven reliable. You'll find both in modern BCDs, though the overall trend is for the large capacity, tec diving BCDs to use the double bag design, and the more compact, mainstream recreational BCDs to use the bladderless design.

Double bag BCDs have an inner bladder protected by an outer shell.

In the days of front mounted BCDs, you wore a separate backpack with harness to hold the cylinder. The backmounted and jacket style BCDs made the backpack as a separate component obsolete, though compact front mounted BCDs remain popular for snorkeling. In Europe, this style of BCD became known as an ABLJ (Adjustable Buoyancy Life Jacket).

**Back Mounted BCDs.** Back mounted BCDs were the first style to integrate all the scuba

components into a single package. Debuting in the 1970s, many back mounted BCDs wrapped large, horse shoe shaped bladders around a conventional backpack. The design had a small, but loyal, following that kept it in the market well into the 1980s. A primary complaint among recreational divers is the tendency for back mounted BCDs to float you face forward at the surface.

Back mounted BCDs could well have died out with the rise of the jacket style BCD were it not for cave divers, who found the configuration optimal for their purposes. In the mid 1980s and into the 1990s, the back mounted BCD evolved for the purposes of cave diving and other forms of tec diving. Today it is the standard BCD used in the tec community, consisting of a high capacity BCD sandwiched between a backplate harness and your doubles. The harness also has a crotch strap, which you rarely find in recreational BCDs anymore. Tec BCDs of this type are commonly referred to as "wings," because the first tec BCDs manufactured used (and still use) this product name.

Despite the wide range of BCDs, there are a number of common features. Some of these are simply conveniences, whereas others are vital to comfort and performance. Some features are nice options for some forms of diving, but something to avoid in other types of diving.

*Inflation/deflation system.* Any modern BCD has a high capacity inflation and deflation system. This most typically takes the form of a large diameter hose with a low pressure inflator (LPI, or "power inflator") system connected to your regulator. Opening the hose valve releases air from your BCD; pressing the LPI button adds air. In some cases, the BCD inflator/deflator assembly integrates an alternate air source second stage that you breathe from in an air-sharing

emergency (more about these later). In some places, divers still use a small, separate cylinder within the BCD for inflation.

Some of the latest BCDs have eliminated the large diameter hose. In its place, you have touch controls that inflate the BCD or release air from an exhaust valve on the BCD shoulder. BCDs with the hose remain the most popular, however.

*Oral inflation hose.* One reason for the hose on your BCD is so that you can inflate it orally, such as might be necessary were you out of air at the surface, or if you had a stuck LPI valve and had to disconnect the inflator hose. BCDs without the large diameter hose have a small diameter oral inflation hose that deploys should you need it.

*Overpressure valve.* All modern BCDs have one or more over pressure valve, which opens before your BCD bursts if you accidentally over inflate it, or if a stuck low pressure inflator over fills it.

Overpressure relief valves are generally set to open when the pressure inside the BCD exceeds the ambient pressure by approximately 2 psi/0.14 bar.

*Quick dump valves.* Higher end BCDs have a rapid exhaust valve that you open by pulling a tether or pulling down on the inflation/deflation hose. This feature is handy because you can release air without having to elevate the hose. ## Milestones in BCD Design

Since their debut in the late 1960s and early 1970s, BCDs have changed dramatically. Along the way, several models became milestones in BCD evolution.

#### **The Waverly UDT Vest**

This vest was not a true BCD, but typical of the surface flotation devices worn by many divers during the 1960s and early 1970s. Of all the vests produced during this period, the

Waverly was among the most popular. For many divers, the Waverly was the first buoyancy device they ever used. It helped teach them about the value of inflatable buoyancy devices — but also revealed some of the shortcomings that future designs would solve.

#### **The Fenzy**

The Fenzy was one of the first true BCDs. Its large-diameter inflation/deflation hose allowed you to use it both at the surface and underwater. Made of heavy duty vulcanized

 The Fenzy introduced many divers to buoyancy adjustment underwater. The French, who made the Fenzy, even coined one of the most widely used term: buoyancy compensator. Soon after the Fenzy's introduction to North America, other manufacturers began producing functionally similar BCDs using less costly methods and materials. This hastened the

acceptance of BCDs. For some time though, the Fenzy maintained a reputation as the prestige BCD.

#### **The At-Pack**

When first introduced in 1972, the At-Pack had some revolutionary features. It was the first unit to integrate a back pack, cylinder, regulator, buoyancy

control device and weights. It was also the first BCD to come with a standard low-pressure inflator. Modern BCDs still incorporate concepts that first appeared on the At-Pack.

#### Alternate Inflator Regulator

The alternate inflator regulator combines your low pressure BCD inflator with an extra second stage. Again, you use this for sharing air. However, since your BCD hose is very

short, the procedure is for the donor to switch to the alternate inflator regulator and give the out-of-air diver the primary. Some divers who use alternate inflator regulators also use an extra long hose on their primary regulators to make air sharing more manageable.

When using an alternate inflator regulator, the donor switches to the alternate and gives the primary regulator from the mouth to the diver who is out of gas.

The extra second stage is typically on a slightly longer hose than the primary in your mouth, and is usually secured in sight in the triangle formed by your chin and the bottom of your rib cage in such a way that it pulls free with a firm tug.

## Sekcja: Tec diving wing/harness - linie 63-64

Tec divers use a similar scuba system, but with some important differences. The tec diver has two completely independent regulators and uses a wing-type BCD with a separate harness. The wing-type BCD sandwiches between the cylinders and the harness; tec BCDs may have two independent bladders and inflation/deflation systems.
